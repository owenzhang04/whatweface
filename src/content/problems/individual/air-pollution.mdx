## What it is

Air pollution is a mix of gases and tiny particles in the air. The most harmful part is fine particulate matter, often called PM2.5: particles so small they get deep into the lungs and can pass into the blood.<Cite source="who-household-air-pollution-factsheet" /> Breathing them raises the risk of stroke, heart disease, lung disease, chest infections and lung cancer.<Cite source="who-ambient-air-quality-factsheet" />

It comes from two places. Outdoor air is polluted by power plants, industry, traffic, burning waste and farming.<Cite source="who-ambient-air-quality-factsheet" /> Indoors, the biggest source is cooking or heating over open fires or simple stoves that burn wood, dung, crop waste, charcoal, coal or kerosene. Women and children, who spend the most time near the fire, breathe the most smoke.<Cite source="who-household-air-pollution-factsheet" />

## How big it is

The Global Burden of Disease study measures how many years of healthy life are lost to each health risk. Particulate matter pollution, outdoor and household combined, accounts for <Stat id="dalys-share" /> of all healthy life lost worldwide. Only high blood pressure is higher, and the two are close enough that either could be first.<Cite source="gbd-2023-risk-factors" />

Household smoke is still common. About <Stat id="no-clean-cooking" /> people cook mainly with polluting fuels and stoves.

## Where it's heading

The harm is falling. Particulate matter used to be the biggest risk to healthy life in the world, accounting for <Stat id="dalys-share-2010" withPeriod />. Since then the number of years lost has dropped by <Stat id="dalys-fall" />, and for a person of a given age the risk has fallen by <Stat id="rate-fall" />.

Exposure is moving in two directions. Exposure to smoke from household fuels has fallen by about <Stat id="household-exposure-fall" /> a year. Exposure to outdoor particles has gone the other way, rising by about <Stat id="ambient-exposure-rise" /> a year.

Clean cooking has stalled. <Stat id="clean-cooking-share" /> of people cook mainly with clean fuels, the same share as the year before. The gap is shrinking across Asia, but in sub-Saharan Africa the number of people without it keeps rising, because the population is growing faster than access.<Cite source="tracking-sdg7-2025" /> At current rates, about <Stat id="no-clean-cooking-2030" /> people would still lack clean cooking by the end of the decade.

## What works

Most outdoor pollution can't be fixed by one person. It takes rules and investment in energy, transport, industry, farming and waste.<Cite source="who-ambient-air-quality-factsheet" /> The World Health Organization (WHO) sets guideline levels for each major pollutant. For fine particles, the guideline for the yearly average is <Stat id="aqg-pm25" />. WHO also sets less strict interim targets, so that highly polluted places can improve step by step.<Cite source="who-air-quality-guidelines-2021" />

Air can get cleaner quickly when countries act. In China, a national clean air plan tightened emission standards for power plants and industry, upgraded industrial boilers, closed outdated factories and pushed cleaner fuels for homes. The average fine particle level people breathed fell from <Stat id="china-2013" withPeriod /> to <Stat id="china-2017" withPeriod />, mostly because of those cuts in emissions rather than changes in the weather.<Cite source="zhang-2019-china-pm25" />

Indoors, the answer is clean fuels and stoves: electricity, gas, biogas, alcohol fuels, solar, or biomass stoves that meet WHO's emission targets. Help with the cost of clean stoves and fuels, better ventilation, and public information all help households switch.<Cite source="who-household-air-pollution-factsheet" />

## More detail

<MoreDetail title="What PM2.5 means">
  PM stands for particulate matter. PM2.5 is the part made of the finest particles, small enough to
  reach deep into the lungs. Levels are measured in micrograms per cubic metre of air. WHO's
  guideline for the yearly average is <Stat id="aqg-pm25" />, and its first, least strict interim
  target is <Stat id="aqg-it1" />.
</MoreDetail>

<MoreDetail title="What the exposure trends measure">
  The Global Burden of Disease study tracks exposure with a summary exposure value. It reflects both
  how common the exposure is and how much harm it causes. The trends on this page are average yearly
  changes in that value over the same years as the other trends, adjusted for changes in the
  population's age.
  <Cite source="gbd-2023-risk-factors" />
</MoreDetail>

<MoreDetail title="How certain are these numbers?">
  The Global Burden of Disease study gives a range for every estimate, shown next to the numbers.
  Air pollution and high blood pressure are close: the study's ranges for their ranks overlap, so
  either could be first. <Cite source="gbd-2023-risk-factors" /> The clean cooking figures are
  estimates that also come with ranges.
  <Cite source="tracking-sdg7-2025" /> The China figures come from a model of emissions and air
  chemistry, checked against ground monitoring stations, not from measurements alone.
  <Cite source="zhang-2019-china-pm25" />
</MoreDetail>
