## What it is

Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing against the walls of your arteries as your heart pumps. When it stays too high, heart attacks, strokes, kidney disease and dementia become more likely.<Cite source="who-hypertension-report-2025" />

High blood pressure usually causes no symptoms. Many people only find out when a health worker measures it, or after serious harm has already happened. That is why it is called a silent killer.<Cite source="who-hypertension-report-2025" />

## How big it is

The Global Burden of Disease study measures how many years of healthy life are lost to each health risk. High blood pressure tops that list. It accounts for <Stat id="dalys-share" /> of all healthy life lost worldwide, just ahead of air pollution and well ahead of smoking and high blood sugar.

The World Health Organization (WHO) counts the people who have hypertension, the medical name for blood pressure that stays high: <Stat id="adults-with-hypertension" withPeriod />. About <Stat id="adults-undiagnosed" /> of them had never been diagnosed.

## Where it's heading

The share of healthy life lost to high blood pressure has grown. It was <Stat id="dalys-share-2010" withPeriod />. The number of years lost rose by <Stat id="dalys-growth" /> between then and the latest estimate, mostly because there are more people and more of them are old.<Cite source="gbd-2023-risk-factors" /> For a person of a given age, the risk has fallen: the age-standardised rate dropped by <Stat id="rate-fall" />.

Control has improved in every WHO region over recent decades, but most people with hypertension still don't have it under control.<Cite source="who-hypertension-report-2025" /> Only <Stat id="treated-share" /> of adults with hypertension are being treated, and <Stat id="adults-controlled" /> people, or <Stat id="controlled-share" />, have their blood pressure under control.

## What works

High blood pressure can be treated with affordable medicines in everyday primary care, the local clinics where most people see a health worker. WHO's HEARTS package helps countries do this with standard treatment plans, reliable access to medicines, team-based care, and systems to track results.<Cite source="who-hypertension-report-2025" />

Some countries have already made large gains. Canada, Costa Rica, Iceland and the Republic of Korea each control blood pressure in more than <Stat id="best-control" /> of the people who have hypertension. WHO estimates that the economic benefits of hypertension control outweigh its costs by about <Stat id="benefit-cost" />.

Eating less salt lowers blood pressure.<Cite source="who-lsss-guideline-2025" /> In trials, people who switched from regular salt to a lower-sodium salt substitute with added potassium saw their systolic pressure fall by <Stat id="salt-substitute-effect" /> on average.

## More detail

<MoreDetail title="What the two blood pressure numbers mean">
  A blood pressure reading has two numbers. The first, systolic pressure, is the pressure while your
  heart beats. The second, diastolic pressure, is the pressure while it rests between beats. WHO
  defines hypertension as a systolic reading of <Stat id="threshold-systolic" /> or more, or a
  diastolic reading of <Stat id="threshold-diastolic" /> or more, measured on two different days.
</MoreDetail>

<MoreDetail title="Why the two sources count different things">
  The Global Burden of Disease study counts harm from any systolic pressure above the lowest-risk
  level, which is lower than the point where doctors diagnose hypertension. Pressure that is raised
  but not yet "hypertension" still causes harm.
  <Cite source="who-hypertension-report-2025" /> WHO's counts of people affected use the medical
  definition instead. So the headline number covers more people than the WHO counts do.
</MoreDetail>

<MoreDetail title="How certain are these numbers?">
  The Global Burden of Disease study gives a range for every estimate, shown next to the numbers.
  High blood pressure and air pollution are close: the study's ranges for their ranks overlap, so
  either could be first.
  <Cite source="gbd-2023-risk-factors" /> WHO's counts of people with hypertension are projections
  from survey-based estimates, and WHO doesn't give a range for them.
</MoreDetail>
